Cup lifespan needs to be extended (e.g. more than 1,000 washing cycles), but adoption will increase as society becomes more sustainability-conscious.

      Research Evidence (SSP1-2.6 Context)

      Research evidence [P] Trucost, 2026

      Increasing the recycling of post-consumer plastics (to 55%) and minimizing landfilling (to a maximum of 10%) could deliver significant environmental benefits. If these targets were implemented across Europe and North America, the environmental cost of plastics could be reduced by over $7.9 billion in net terms, accounting for the increased environmental impacts of waste collection and management, and in addition to the direct economic gains from the recovered value of recycled plastics and recovered energy (Trucost, 2026).

      Research evidence [P] Steilemann, 2022

      The chemical and plastics industries account for around 7% of global greenhouse gas emissions. By 2030 this could reach a level equivalent to around 300 new coal-fired power plants. Plastics are made by taking crude oil out of the ground and processing it, which accounts for 4–8% of annual global oil consumption. By 2050, according to experts, growing demand for plastics could push this figure as high as 20% (Steilemann, 2022).

      SCENARIO P3 • SSP3-7.0

      SSP3-7.0 (“Regional Rivalry” / Fragmented High-Emissions Infrastructure)

      [P] Narrative (assumption): Under SSP3-7.0, a fragmented, high-emissions global economy increases climate risks across coffee-producing regions. Reduced coffee yields contribute to higher coffee bean prices and lower operating margins for cafés and bakeries. To maintain profitability, operators may extend cup end-of-life beyond recommended replacement thresholds rather than purchasing replacement cups. This increases surface micro-degradation and may raise consumer hygiene concerns, reducing user adoption and constraining partner network growth.
      [P] Scenario Assumption: Coffee bean prices increase by approximately 80% under severe climate stress. This is a scenario assumption informed by drought-related coffee market observations and must not be interpreted as a direct mathematical consequence of reported land-suitability losses. Do not derive the price increase mathematically from land suitability loss alone.
      [P] Method note: Climate scenario narratives can be developed qualitatively using the storyline approach of Shepherd et al. (2018). The narratives on this page are assumptions, not outputs of that method. https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10584-018-2317-9

      Research Evidence (SSP3-7.0 Coffee & Climate Context)

      Research evidence [P] Abigaba et al., 2024

      Shrinking suitable land: rising temperatures and shifting rainfall mean that up to 50% of current land suitable for growing high-grade Arabica coffee could become unusable by 2050. Massive land suitability losses: modelling for 2050 under SSP3-7.0 shows a 20% reduction in land suitable for Arabica coffee and a 9% reduction for Robusta globally (Abigaba et al., 2024) https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10457-024-01025-3

      Research evidence [P] The Guardian, 2026

      Extreme heat and “coffee-harming” days: major producers like Brazil, Vietnam, Colombia, Ethiopia and Indonesia have experienced dozens of additional days annually with temperatures exceeding 30°C (86°F), the upper threshold for Arabica health (The Guardian, 2026).

      Research evidence [P] Univ. of Waterloo, 2024

      Premium specialty Arabicas are projected to experience the sharpest price hikes and could become a luxury good, while commercial roasters increasingly substitute them with cheaper, marginally more resilient Robusta beans. The current market price for Arabica beans, which make up around 70 per cent of the coffee market, is roughly $2.70 US per pound, against around $1.49 US a year earlier. Coffee commodity prices are up roughly 80 per cent during the drought. Using drought impact as the climate analogue for extreme climate change under SSP3-7.0, the projected price increase will be more than 80 per cent (Univ. of Waterloo, 2024) https://uwaterloo.ca/climate-institute/news/climate-change-brewing-your-coffees-price-surge-explained
