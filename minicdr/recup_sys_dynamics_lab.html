Case Study • Circular Takeaway Infrastructure

Germany's RECUP Deposit-Return Cup System

Founded in 2016 in Munich, RECUP operates Germany's largest reusable deposit-return system for takeaway coffee and food. By pooling standardized, dishwasher-safe cups across independent cafés, bakeries, and chains, RECUP turns single-use packaging costs into a shared circular network.

Founded & Scale
2016 • Munich

Expanded nationwide to 20,000+ partner outlets and 100,000+ active users across Germany.

Deposit Mechanism
€1.00 Deposit

Low-friction €1 refundable deposit step encourages impulse takeaway adoption while incentivizing eventual return.

Material & Durability
Up to 1,000×

Made of 100% recyclable polypropylene (PP); rated for up to 1,000 rinse cycles (500+ cycles cited in brand award benchmarks).

LCA Break-Even
> 50 Uses

Environmentally outperforms disposable single-use cups once a cup exceeds roughly 50 reuse cycles.

How RECUP Works — 4-Step Operational Cycle

Standardized reverse logistics across any participating partner outlet

Borrow → Use → Return (€1) → Wash
01

Borrow (€1 Deposit)

Customers borrow a reusable container for a €1 deposit at a participating partner café or restaurant.

02

Takeaway Use

Customers use the lightweight, BPA-free polypropylene cup or bowl for takeaway food or hot/cold drinks.

03

Return & Refund

Customers return it to any designated partner collection point for professional washing and get their €1 deposit back.

04

Sanitize & Re-Circulate

Once commercially washed and inspected on site, the cup re-enters active stock ready for another takeaway order.

System Dynamics Behind the Case

N1 → N2 Partner Density & Network Effects

A reusable cup only works if customers do not have to walk out of their way to return it. High Partner Density (N1) creates ubiquitous drop-off points, raising Return Convenience (N2) and unlocking two-sided network adoption.

N3 → N4 Vendor Economics

Partners pay a flat monthly system fee to RECUP. As Adoption Volume (N3) climbs, cafés avoid purchasing thousands of disposable paper/plastic cups and lids—generating net Vendor Savings (N4) that attract additional outlets.

N6 −→ N5 Cup Hoarding & Stock Leakage (B1)

Because the €1 deposit is low enough to forget in a car, office desk, or kitchen cupboard, rapid usage spikes trigger Cup Hoarding (N6). Unreturned cups drain Circulating Stock (N5), forcing emergency cup re-orders or throttling partner availability.

German Packaging Policy Context (Verified Timeline)

Regulatory tailwinds driving reusable container adoption in Germany

Dates Verified • Primary Legal Sources
1 Jan 2003 Mandatory Single-Use Beverage Deposit (Einwegpfand)

Under the German Packaging Ordinance (Verpackungsverordnung, VerpackV), a mandatory deposit (typically €0.25) took effect on ecologically disadvantageous single-use beverage containers, establishing deposit-return habits nationwide.

1 Jan 2019 German Packaging Act (Verpackungsgesetz, VerpackG)

Replaced the 1998 Packaging Ordinance, introducing stricter recycling quotas, mandatory producer registration with the Central Agency Packaging Register (ZSVR / LUCID), and explicit reusable-packaging targets.

1 Jan 2022 Universal Single-Use Plastic Bottle & Can Deposit

Amended VerpackG extended the mandatory €0.25 deposit to virtually all single-use plastic beverage bottles and beverage cans (0.1–3.0 L) regardless of contents (milk-based drinks in plastic bottles phased in on 1 Jan 2024).

1 Jan 2023 Mandatory Reusable Offer Obligation (Mehrwegangebotspflicht)

Under §§ 33–34 VerpackG, takeaway food and beverage vendors must offer a reusable packaging alternative at no higher price or worse conditions than single-use packaging (small businesses with ≤5 staff and ≤80 m² are exempt from stocking a pool system, provided they fill customer-brought containers).

Systems Thinking Foundations • Graph & Network Theory

What is a Causal Loop Diagram (CLD)?

A visual language for mapping system architecture. CLDs illuminate how interconnected variables generate systemic behaviors through feedback rather than linear cause-and-effect. Formally, a CLD is a directed, signed, and weighted graph G = (V, E) where vertices (V) are system state variables and directed edges (E) represent causal links carrying polarity (+ / −) and link strength.

Vertices (V) 01

Variables (System States)

Quantities, conditions, or feelings that fluctuate over time (e.g., Usage, Cost, Trust, Cup Hoarding). Variables must be quantifiable or directionally variable—never verbs or static events.

Edge Sign (+) 02

Positive Link Polarity (+)

Indicates change in the same direction. If Variable X increases, Variable Y increases above baseline. If X decreases, Y decreases below baseline (all else equal).

Edge Sign (−) 03

Negative Link Polarity (−)

Indicates change in the opposite direction. If Variable X increases, Variable Y decreases below baseline, creating counter-forces and self-limiting constraints.

Cycles (R / B) 04

Closed Feedback Loops

When causal chains wrap back onto themselves, creating non-linear dynamic behavior: either self-reinforcing exponential growth/decay (R) or goal-seeking stability (B).

Reinforcing (R) vs. Balancing (B) Loops

Reinforcing Loop (R) — Even Number of (−) Links R

Contains zero or an even number of negative (−) links. Every pass around the loop compounds the initial change—producing virtuous exponential growth or vicious collapse (e.g., RECUP R1).

Balancing Loop (B) — Odd Number of (−) Links B

Contains an odd number of negative (−) links. An initial increase returns as a downward pressure, stabilizing the system or capping growth (e.g., RECUP B1 via N6 −→ N5).

How to Trace a Shock Step-by-Step

  1. Pick a starting variable & shock sign: Perturb one node with an increase (+) or decrease (−).
  2. Multiply signs across each directed edge:
    (+) × (+) = (+)
    (+) × (−) = (−)
    (−) × (−) = (+)
  3. Compare the returning signal: If the signal arrives back at the starting node with the same sign, the loop is Reinforcing (R); if flipped, it is Balancing (B).

Rapid Prototyping

Turning a conceptual causal loop diagram into a working interactive prototype using structured prompts.

Why Use It in Systems Thinking

A static CLD shows structure but not behaviour. A quick interactive prototype lets you drag, highlight loops and trigger changes, so you can test whether your causal logic says what you think it says.

Three-Step Workflow

1

Define the system and layout first.

List nodes, links, polarities and loops on paper before prompting.

Nodes (N1–N6) + Signs (+/−) + Loops (R/B)
2

Prompt for a single-file prototype.

Ask for one HTML file with D3 and JavaScript, name every node and link, and state the buttons you need.

Single HTML/SVG + D3.js + Interactive Controls
3

Human-in-the-loop audit.

Check every link, polarity and loop against your design. Test highlighting, clearing and propagation, then ask the AI to fix what is wrong.

Verify Wiring → Test Shocks → Refine
