Critiquing Sustainability Claims Using Theoretical Lenses

Final teaching demonstration activity examining how thermodynamic theory and boundary-explicit quantitative modelling test popular circularity and reuse claims—moving students from surface-level sustainability metrics to rigorous structural critique.

§

Pedagogical Purpose & Critical Learning Objectives

Thermodynamic & Boundary Audit

This module equips students to interrogate sustainability claims rather than accept headline numbers at face value:

  • 1. Sustainability claims depend on assumptions: headline targets embed operational, behavioral, and system-level premises.
  • 2. Models depend on chosen system boundaries: shifting spatial, temporal, or lifecycle boundaries alters conclusions.
  • 3. Theoretical frameworks expose hidden assumptions: thermodynamics surfaces physical constraints obscured by monetary or linear accounting.
  • 4. Recycling and reuse face physical limits: material degradation, dissipation, and operational energy constrain circularity.
  • 5. Critical evaluation over uncritical acceptance: sustainability interventions require empirical sensitivity checks and theoretical scrutiny.
Theoretical Foundation • Nicholas Georgescu-Roegen

Entropy and the Economic Process

Purpose: Introduce Georgescu-Roegen’s critique of conventional economic thinking.

Thermodynamic Irreversibility & Entropy

  • Any transformation of matter or energy causes an increase in entropy.
  • In theory entropy can remain constant only in reversible processes.
  • Real-world transformations are irreversible.
  • All irreversible processes increase entropy.

Critique of Circularity & Throughput

  • Georgescu-Roegen criticised the notion that economic systems function as a perfectly circular process.
  • Economic activity depends upon continual throughput of low-entropy matter and energy.
Core Analytical Takeaway

Conventional circularity diagrams often depict materials cycling endlessly within a closed loop, overlooking that every physical transformation converts low-entropy availability into high-entropy dissipation and waste heat.

Material Entropy & Circularity Limits

Multi-Cycle Recycling Decay

Purpose: Illustrate Georgescu-Roegen’s concept of material entropy.

  • Recycling slows depletion but does not eliminate degradation: extending material residence time delays exhaustion but cannot reverse physical dispersion.
  • Every productive operation degrades matter: sorting, remelting, mechanical processing, and use alter material structure and lose recoverable mass.
  • Georgescu-Roegen’s proposed Fourth Law of Thermodynamics: matter, like energy, experiences progressive qualitative degradation and dissipation in closed systems (a subject of ongoing thermodynamic debate, used here as a theoretical lens).
  • Downward spiral rather than perfect circularity: repeated recycling moves materials toward lower availability and degraded functionality rather than perpetual equivalence.

System Boundary Expansion & Multi-Indicator Trade-Offs

Why Peer-Reviewed Studies Still Support a ~20–50 Use Benchmark

End-of-Life Paper Cup Recycling Credits

Foteinis (2020) found that reusable cups break even against landfilled paper cups at 21 uses, but require 41 uses when the single-use paper cups receive recycling credits.

Multi-Indicator LCA Trade-Offs

The UNEP Life Cycle Initiative (2021) meta-analysis of 10 cup LCAs reports break-even ranges starting around 10 uses for climate and fossil energy, while requiring higher reuse counts to offset dishwashing water use and freshwater/marine eutrophication.

Return-Rate Policy Thresholds

Modelling by Eunomia / TOMRA indicates that citywide deposit cup systems require at least an 83% return rate (~6 average rotations) to outperform single-use paper cups on greenhouse gas emissions.

Seminar Reflection & Critical Inquiry

Discussion Questions

  1. Which assumption has the greatest influence on the break-even point?

    Test how shifts in Avoided Paper Cup Energy (Edisp), Wash & Transport Energy (Ewash, Etrans), and Per-Cycle Attrition (r) shrink the denominator Edisp − Ewash − Etrans − (r · Emfg) toward the tipping point where break-even becomes non-viable or shifts sharply upward.

  2. Which assumptions can RECUP influence?

    Examine cup weight and polymer durability (Emfg), deposit incentives and partner return workflows (r), washing partner efficiency (Ewash), and collection/redistribution route consolidation (Etrans).

  3. Which assumptions lie outside RECUP’s control?

    Consider consumer hoarding or unreturned cups outside deposit enforcement, regional electricity grid intensity, municipal recycling/incineration infrastructure, and baseline manufacturing characteristics of rival disposable cups.

  4. Why might different studies report different break-even values?

    Compare primary-energy-only boundaries (~9–11 uses) against expanded boundaries incorporating cup attrition (r), end-of-life paper recycling credits (Foteinis, 2020: 21 vs. 41 uses), multi-indicator trade-offs such as water and eutrophication (UNEP Life Cycle Initiative, 2021), and return-rate thresholds (Eunomia / TOMRA).
